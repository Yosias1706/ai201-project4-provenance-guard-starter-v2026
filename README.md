## What This Does

<!-- The path a submission takes, from arriving to the label a reader sees.
     Six or seven steps — this is the trace you wrote in Milestone 1. -->

Provenance Guard takes a piece of writing, estimates whether it was written by a person or generated by AI, and tells the writer in plain language — with a way to appeal if they think it got it wrong. The full Milestone 1 trace, including the false-positive walk-through and the endpoint contracts, is in [trace.md](trace.md).

1. **Arrives at `POST /submit`** (`app.py::submit`) as JSON with `text` and `creator_id`.
2. **Input check.** Missing/blank `text` or `creator_id`, a body that isn't a JSON object, or text over 20,000 characters is refused with a 4xx and a JSON `error` + `message`. Nothing bad reaches the signals.
3. **Gets a `content_id`** (a UUID) — the handle the log and the appeal path use to find this decision again.
4. **Signal one — `detector.py::model_signal`.** gpt2 runs locally and measures how predictable the text is → `model_score` (0–1, higher = more AI-like). Text too short to score is refused with a 400.
5. **Signal two — `stylometry.py::style_signal`.** Measures the shape of the text — sentence-length spread and expressive punctuation → `style_score` (0–1, higher = more AI-like).
6. **Combine — `scoring.py::combine_signals`.** Weighted average (0.6 model / 0.4 style), pulled toward 0.5 when the signals strongly disagree, and capped below the AI band unless both signals agree → `confidence`.
7. **Label — `scoring.py::score_to_label`.** Maps the score to `human` / `unsure` / `ai` and the full sentence the writer reads.
8. **Logged, then answered.** `audit.log_decision` appends one JSON line to `logs/audit.jsonl` with both signal scores, the combined score, the guess, the label and `status: "decided"`; the response returns the same fields plus the `content_id`.

If the writer disagrees, `POST /appeal` (`app.py::appeal`) checks the `content_id` exists in the log (404 if not), appends an appeal entry with `status: "under_review"` and their reasoning next to the original decision, and confirms.

## Signals and Scoring

<!-- For each signal: what property it measures, why that might differ between
     human and AI writing, and WHAT IT CAN'T SEE.

     The blind spot is not optional. If you can't name it you don't understand
     the signal yet — and unit 8's attack set is built out of blind spots. -->

### Signal one — the model signal

`detector.py::model_signal`

**What it measures:** How predictable the text is to gpt2, running locally. It computes perplexity (how surprised the model is by each next token) and squashes it onto 0–1 so that low perplexity gives a high score. Higher = more AI-like.

**Why that might differ between human and AI writing:** A language model writes by repeatedly picking a likely next word, so text a model wrote is text a model finds easy to predict. People wander more: specific details, odd word choices, sentences that turn somewhere unexpected.

**What it can't see:** Who actually wrote the text. Predictability isn't authorship. Plain writing — common words in short, ordinary sentences, the way someone writing in a second language or a younger writer often writes — is predictable too and scores like AI. It also fails the other way: AI text that uses rare or ornate vocabulary surprises the model and scores human. It has no idea about meaning or whether a person edited the output.

### Signal two — the style signal

`stylometry.py::style_signal`

**What it measures:** The shape of the text, without reading it. Three measures: how much sentence length varies (coefficient of variation), how much of the vocabulary is unique (type-token ratio over a fixed-size window), and how often the writer uses "expressive" punctuation — dashes, semicolons, colons, parentheses, question/exclamation marks — per sentence.

**Why that might differ between human and AI writing:** Generated prose tends toward even, mid-length sentences, safe repeated vocabulary, and tidy comma-and-period punctuation. Human writing is lumpier: a three-word sentence next to a forty-word one, an aside in parentheses, a dash where a comma would do.

**What it can't see:** Anything the text says. It can't read. Anything that changes the shape without changing the substance moves it — typos, breaking sentences up, pasting in a quote, sprinkling in a few dashes. That is cheap to do on purpose. It's also weak on short text: two or three sentences give almost no spread to measure.

### The combining rule

<!-- Write the rule in words, then give the numbers. What happens when the two
     signals disagree? -->

**The rule:** (written down before I coded it)

1. Start with a weighted average, with the model signal counting more than the style signal. The style signal can't read and is cheap to push around, so it shouldn't be able to carry a decision on its own.
2. If the two signals are far apart, that disagreement *is* uncertainty — pull the score halfway back toward 0.5.
3. "High-confidence AI" needs **both** signals to agree. If either one is below the agreement floor, the combined score is capped just under the AI threshold, so the worst a split can produce is "unsure". This is the rule that protects plain human writing.

**The numbers:** (all in `config.py`)

| Setting | Value |
|---|---|
| `WEIGHT_MODEL_SIGNAL` | 0.6 |
| `WEIGHT_STYLE_SIGNAL` | 0.4 |
| `DISAGREEMENT_GAP` — gap that triggers the pull toward 0.5 | 0.40 (score becomes `0.5 + (score − 0.5) × 0.5`) |
| `AGREEMENT_FLOOR` — both signals must be at least this for "AI" | 0.65 (otherwise capped at `AI_THRESHOLD − 0.01` = 0.69) |
| `HUMAN_THRESHOLD` | 0.40 |
| `AI_THRESHOLD` | 0.70 |

Inside the style signal (`stylometry.py`): sentence-length spread 0.55, punctuation 0.45, type-token ratio **0.0**. TTR ran backwards on my calibration texts — the clearly-AI paragraph had the most varied vocabulary (MATTR 0.92) and my plain human paragraph the least (0.78) — so I kept computing and printing it but gave it no weight.

I also moved signal one's curve in `detector.py::model_signal` from midpoint 45 / steepness 1.6 to **midpoint 28 / steepness 2.5**. At 45, every calibration text scored above 0.5 on the model signal, including a clearly human paragraph (perplexity 31.3 → 0.64). My texts ran from perplexity 19 to 40, so the old midpoint sat outside the range where the decisions actually happen.

**Where it lives:** `scoring.py::combine_signals`

**Calibration run** (both signals, after the changes above):

| Input | Perplexity | model_score | style_score | combined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Clearly AI (generic essay on AI) | 19.3 | 0.717 | 0.876 | **0.781** |
| Clearly human (grandmother's button jar) | 31.3 | 0.431 | 0.064 | **0.285** |
| Formal human (committee report) | 39.8 | 0.293 | 0.498 | **0.375** |
| Lightly-edited AI (remote work, with "honestly saves me about an hour a day" added) | 24.1 | 0.593 | 0.765 | **0.662** |
| Plain human (short sentences about a library job) | 25.6 | 0.556 | 0.961 | **0.609** |

Clearly-AI and clearly-human land about 0.50 apart.

**A case where my two signals split, and what my rule does with it:**

The plain human paragraph — *"I started working at the library last year. The job is not hard but it is busy. Every day I help people find books and use the computers…"* Signal one is unsure about it (0.556). Signal two is almost certain it's AI (0.961): every sentence is 7–10 words long and there isn't a single dash, colon or question mark. A plain 0.6/0.4 average gives 0.718, which would have been **high-confidence AI** — a real writer accused, exactly the person from my Milestone 1 trace. With my rule, the 0.405 gap pulls it to 0.609, and because the model signal is below the 0.65 floor it could never have crossed 0.69 anyway. It lands in "unsure". The cost: lightly-edited AI (0.662) also lands in "unsure" instead of "AI", because the model signal reads it at 0.593. I'd rather miss that than accuse the library paragraph.

## Label Variants

<!-- The exact text of all three labels, as a reader would see them, and the
     score range each covers.

     Write them for someone who has never heard the word "threshold". -->

| Label | Score range | The exact text a reader sees |
|---|---|---|
| high-confidence human | 0.00 – under 0.40 | We think this was most likely written by a person. There's nothing you need to do. |
| unsure | 0.40 – under 0.70 | Our automated check couldn't tell whether this was written by a person or with help from AI. This is not an accusation, and nothing happens to your work because of it. Lots of writing people do entirely on their own ends up here. If you'd like a person to take a look, you can ask for a review. |
| high-confidence AI | 0.70 – 1.00 | Our automated check found strong signs that this text was generated by AI. This is a guess made by software, not proof, and it can be wrong. If you wrote this yourself, you can appeal and a person will review it. |

The text lives in `scoring.py` (`LABEL_HUMAN`, `LABEL_UNSURE`, `LABEL_AI`); the ranges come from `HUMAN_THRESHOLD` and `AI_THRESHOLD` in `config.py`.

All three are reachable — I submitted text that produced each one:

| Input | confidence | guess |
|---|---|---|
| Generic essay on AI | 0.7806 | `ai` |
| Grandmother's button jar | 0.2673 | `human` |
| Plain paragraph about a library job | 0.609 | `unsure` |

**Why I worded the "unsure" one this way:**

The person most likely to see it is the one from my false-positive trace: someone who wrote every word themselves in plain, simple sentences. My first draft was just *"Our checker couldn't tell whether this was written by a person or by AI."* Read as the writer, that sounds like "we suspect you", and it leaves the obvious questions unanswered — is my work hidden? do I have to prove something? So the final version says outright that it isn't an accusation, that nothing happens to their work, that lots of people's own writing lands here, and that they can ask a person to look if they want. I deliberately didn't say *why* writing lands there ("simple writing", "short sentences"), because that reads as a comment on how well they write.

## How I Used AI

**Moment 1 — testing my false-positive number on someone it would hit**

- *What I asked for:* I was working on my own, so I pasted criterion 1 ("of 10 pieces of my own writing, at most 1 is labeled high-confidence AI") into Claude and asked it to write the message a real writer would send me if they were one of the people that rate allows — without telling me whether the number was right.
- *What came back:* A short, angry message from someone whose essay got flagged: they'd written it themselves, they write plainly because English isn't their first language, they had no idea what the score meant, and they had no way to prove a negative. The line that stuck was that "1 in 10" sounds small until you're the one.
- *What I changed:* I kept the number — I can't honestly promise 0 with a perplexity signal — but I made it hold in **each** of the three trials instead of on average, and I took the "plain writing, second language" person into Milestone 4 as a calibration input. That's what led to the agreement floor in `scoring.py::combine_signals`: without it, my plain library paragraph scored 0.718 and would have been labeled AI.

**Moment 2 — reading my labels as the writer**

- *What I asked for:* I pasted my three label variants into Claude and asked it, answering as the writer and not as an engineer, what each one would make me think it was saying about me and what I'd do next, without rewriting them.
- *What came back:* The human and AI labels read about how I meant them. The unsure one didn't: my draft was just "Our checker couldn't tell whether this was written by a person or by AI", and the answer read it as "they suspect me but won't say so" and asked whether the piece would be hidden and whether they had to prove anything.
- *What I changed:* I rewrote the unsure label myself to say it isn't an accusation, that nothing happens to the work, that lots of people's own writing lands there, and that they can ask a person to look. I also added "This is a guess made by software, not proof" to the AI label so it can't be read as a final verdict.
